## Real-iPhone result and rejection

The owner tested the eight positions in this order:

1. low E;
2. A;
3. D;
4. G;
5. B;
6. high E;
7. E-major chord; and
8. rest.

The owner reported:

`Only the G string the A string and the high E string played anything that I could hear the low E string the D string, and the beast string made no noise that I could hear`

Accepted interpretation of that report:

1. A, G, and high E were audible.
2. Low E, D, and B were not audible to the owner on the real iPhone.
3. The six-anchor sample set failed real-device acceptance.
4. Numerical RMS equality, valid hashes, source-start selection, and successful hosted read-back did not predict perceptual audibility through the real device.
5. No claim is made that the inaudible files contained no digital signal; the failure is that they did not produce usable audible output in the bounded real-device test.

## What remains valid evidence

This experiment established useful but non-integrated evidence:

1. sampled playback can consume the accepted semantic position and timing models;
2. chord voices can be scheduled at one onset;
3. rests can remain silent outcomes;
4. the two-second delay and accepted first-audition focus behavior can coexist with a sampled engine;
5. source extraction must recognize valid notes beginning at sample zero;
6. raw signal and RMS checks are insufficient as acceptance substitutes for perceptual audibility; and
7. audio assets must be transported and locked as intact files rather than reconstructed through fragile text-oriented routes.

None of that evidence authorizes inheritance of the sampled implementation.
